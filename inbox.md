- [Millennium Problems for biology](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/samuel-g-rodriques-080a9b22_in-light-of-the-progress-in-mathematics-share-7507215438561427456-C1Sc/)
  Samuel G. Rodriques (Edison Scientific, FutureHouse), LinkedIn, around 2026-09-19. A set of hard biology problems modelled on the Millennium Prize Problems, published at millenniumproblems.bio, led with Michaela Hinks. He frames it explicitly as a response to what has happened in mathematics, and calls it "the 'last reasonable eval' for AI in biology".
  The selection criteria are the interesting part and they are all about verification. Problems had to be "very hard to solve but very easy to validate in a simple laboratory environment", checkable in a standard wet lab in days to weeks, gradeable against an objective measurable endpoint, and understandable to someone with a basic biology background. Anything without a clear endpoint was excluded, ageing named as the example. Reviewers credited: Adam Marblestone, Michael Baym, Erika DeBenedictis, Tony Kulesa. 567 reactions and 37 comments when read.
  Relevance to the beat. This is the benchmark question moving from mathematics into biology, and it arrives in the same weeks as the argument about whether benchmarks are the right target at all. Edition 025 covered the declaration by 25 Fields Medallists calling the benchmark-driven approach "severely misaligned" with mathematics, and Tao's SAIR initiative in this inbox deliberately targets everyday mathematical work rather than benchmark problem-solving. Rodriques is moving the other way, building a benchmark on purpose, and the justification is that biology can validate an answer in a lab in a fortnight while mathematics has spent this month arguing about how a proof gets checked and credited. Whether cheap validation makes benchmarks safe, or just makes the same incentive problems arrive faster, is the question the edition can put next to the mathematics thread.

- [ScientistTwo: Pioneering the Human Knowledge Frontier with Autonomous AI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19644)
  arXiv:2609.19644, submitted 2026-09-17. Authors: Jaehyun Nam, Jinsung Yoon, Yanzhou Pan, Yubo Wang, Rui Meng, Parthasarathy Ranganathan, Tomas Pfister. Surfaced via a DAIR.AI post on LinkedIn (linkedin.com/posts/banger-paper-from-google-cloud-ai-research-share-7506910023642529792-jFRi) which attributes it to Google Cloud AI Research, though the arXiv page itself lists no affiliations.
  A multi-agent framework that takes a problem from a human expert and runs the whole cycle with no further intervention: establishes baselines, forms hypotheses, runs experiments, does ablations, then drafts the paper and puts it through a "closed-loop simulated peer-review rebuttal engine". The claims are large and stated plainly in the abstract: solutions that "consistently outperform human state-of-the-art models", papers that "achieve higher average review ratings than human-authored papers under automated AI review agents", and the framing that it is "not merely an assistive tool but an autonomous scientific pioneer".
  Relevance to the beat, and the part worth being careful about. The evaluation is the story. Papers are graded by AI reviewers against papers accepted at ICLR, ICML and NeurIPS, so the headline result is that an AI writes papers an AI prefers. The abstract does not claim human reviewers were involved. Commenters on the post raised the other half, whether the system expands a frontier or optimises inside a problem someone else already framed, which is the same objection the Fields Medallists made in edition 025 about benchmark-driven work: "could destroy fertile ground instead of breathing life into new ideas". It also sits directly against the Rodriques biology Millennium Problems already in this inbox, where the whole selection rule was that a human can validate the answer in a lab in a fortnight. One project builds AI-graded benchmarks, the other builds lab-validated ones, in the same month.

Whether any human evaluation was run at all, and if so how many papers and reviewers. What "outperform human state-of-the-art" is measured on, and on how many problems. Whether the generated papers are public. And whether the paper acknowledges the AI-reviewing-AI circularity anywhere, since that is what the edition should say plainly either way.

- [The AI discourse in academia is toxic](https://blekhman.substack.com/p/the-ai-discourse-in-academia-is-toxic)
  Ran Blekhman, Substack, 2026-09-20. An account of what happened when he posted about the Miao et al. Nature paper on turning papers into MCP servers, the Paper2Agent work already in this inbox. He reports hundreds of vitriolic replies including personal attacks, xenophobia, and calls for him to lose funding or leave his job. He also cites Alexander Kustov, a political scientist, receiving "professional threats, ad hominem, and coordinated pile-ons" for an AI-positive piece.
  His argument. "The specific tool, application, or question all disappear into a general presumption that the whole category is terrible." He holds that "the only serious position on AI in science is nuanced and case by case; any answer to 'is AI good for science' that neatly fits into a single yes or no is unserious", and that "a software tool for pattern recognition has been recoded into a morality test". The consequence he names is selection rather than persuasion: "when the response to a mildly AI-positive share is a toxic pileup, people learn: they stop posting about AI."
  Relevance to the beat. This is the first item in the inbox about the conditions the argument is happening under rather than the argument itself, and it is about a paper already covered here. It also bears on a question raised in edition 025 and again by Gowers: if the visible discourse is selecting for people willing to absorb a pile-on, then what can be read publicly is not a fair sample of what the field thinks. Worth pairing with the Fields Medallists' declaration and Gowers's dissent, both of which were careful, named and civil, as a contrast with what Blekhman describes happening to people without that standing.

- [Theory Is All You Need: AI, Human Cognition, and Causal Reasoning](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4737265)
  Teppo Felin (Utah State, Huntsman School of Business) and Matthias Holweg (Oxford, Said Business School). SSRN preprint, published in Strategy Science 9(4), 2024, doi 10.1287/stsc.2024.0189. Note this is from 2024 and is not new, so it belongs in an edition as background rather than as news.
  The argument. AI is good at prediction from data and is backward looking and imitative, while human cognition works through theory-based causal reasoning that is forward looking and can produce genuine novelty. The mechanism they name is that a theory lets a person intervene in the world and run directed experiments to generate data that does not yet exist. They introduce "data-belief asymmetries" for the gap, and use heavier-than-air flight as the worked example, a case where the existing data argued against the thing that turned out to be possible.
  Relevance to the beat. It is the theoretical version of the objection running through the last several editions. The Fields Medallists said benchmark-driven AI "could destroy fertile ground instead of breathing life into new ideas", Gowers argued the risk is losing the people rather than the results, and the ScientistTwo paper in this inbox claims autonomous end-to-end discovery graded by AI reviewers. Felin and Holweg give the reason those worries might be structural rather than temporary: if novelty comes from theory-driven intervention rather than from extrapolating existing data, then a system trained on the record cannot by construction do the part that matters. Useful as the framing paragraph for an edition that covers autonomous research agents, rather than as an item in a list.
