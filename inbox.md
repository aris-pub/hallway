- [AlphaGenome 2: deciphering how cells read DNA to better understand disease](https://deepmind.google/blog/alphagenome-2-deciphering-how-cells-read-dna-to-better-understand-disease/)
  Google DeepMind released AlphaGenome 2, a model whose inputs are a 1-Mb DNA sequence and a target tissue, cell type or cell state, and whose outputs are predictions of gene regulation and variant effects in that setting. It was trained on human and mouse single-cell atlases and bulk functional genomics data, and it can be applied to new single-cell datasets without fine-tuning. It also predicts DNA methylation. The preprint (Gayoso, Mottram, Karollus and colleagues, with Hassabis, Kohli and Avsec as senior authors: https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/DeepMind.com/Blog/alphagenome-2-deciphering-how-cells-read-dna-to-better-understand-disease/alphagenome-2.pdf) reports that it doubles recall of causal single-cell eQTLs at 90 percent precision compared with earlier models. It also comes with a precomputed atlas of enhancer-gene links and scores for 7 million common variants. Access is through an API for non-commercial research. Found through a LinkedIn post by Amanda Stafford (https://www.linkedin.com/posts/amanda-stafford-phd-059325152_today-we-are-excited-to-launch-alphagenome-share-7526304596818837504-W9Ny/). For the beat, the angle is how researchers get access to it: a closed model behind a non-commercial API with a precomputed atlas, and AlphaGenome is one of the databases in the Antigravity science plugin covered in No. 032. - [PaperRaven: a machine assistant for generating topic-bounded, verified samples of the scientific literature](https://aaronclauset.github.io/paperraven/)
  Aaron Clauset released PaperRaven (v0.2.0, BSD 3-Clause), a literature survey tool run with Claude Code (announcement thread: https://bsky.app/profile/aaronclauset.bsky.social/post/3n24tfuhgbc2i). The researcher describes a topic and picks a few seed papers, and the tool drafts inclusion and exclusion criteria that the researcher refines. In each round Claude proposes papers, ordinary code checks each one against OpenAlex and Crossref and refuses any it cannot find, and a separate model judges each paper against the criteria and writes down why. It searches from the model's recall and the web first, then backward through what the corpus cites, then forward through what cites it. The output is a bibliography, an audit trail of every decision and who made it, and an interactive citation network. In his example (culture and social learning in whales and dolphins), 197 papers were proposed, 5 refused as unfindable, and 158 kept after 42 rounds. For the beat, the angle is the design: the model proposes, deterministic code verifies against a public record, and every decision keeps its reason, which is a concrete answer to hallucinated references. Curation agent to confirm the version, licence and example-run numbers against the project page.

- [AI is writing more research code. Who is reviewing it?](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/impactevaluations/ai-is-writing-more-research-code---who-is-reviewing-it--)
  A post on the World Bank's Development Impact blog (November 3 2026) by Ankriti Singh, María Reyes Retana Torre and Maria Jones of the DIME team. The authors argue that coding agents make research code cheap to produce, so review of that code matters more. They cite figures on how often code is wrong even when it runs: only one in five replication packages in their own checks reproduced as submitted, and an audit of 110 economics and political science articles found substantive coding errors in 26, even though 85 percent were reproducible. Their team turned its peer code review checklists into a public agent skill (https://github.com/dime-worldbank/coding-agents-hub/tree/main/skills-library/ai-review-code) with more than 60 checks. The review runs in two phases: a read-only pass that reports findings by severity, then changes that the researcher approves. The post gives examples of errors that produce no error message, such as an m:m merge, a forced duplicate drop, and a comment that claims village-level clustering when the code uses robust standard errors. In a test package with these three planted errors, the authors report that all three were flagged as high severity. Pairs with the Baker et al. preprint in the inbox, where 30 agents produced 18 different pipelines for one study. - [Down the Rabbit Hole of LLM Peer Review](https://radiologyai.substack.com/p/down-the-rabbit-hole-of-llm-peer)
  A post (November 11 2026) by Bastien Le Guellec and Su Hwan Kim, the authors of the "Major Revision, Minor Accountability" preprint that No. 032 covered (arxiv.org/abs/2610.31414). The post appears on a Substack tied to the journal Radiology: Artificial Intelligence. It adds a result not described in the No. 032 item: telling the models in a single sentence that a manuscript had already been revised once raised acceptances from 4 to 18 percent and cut major revisions from 70 to 42 percent, and for Gemini 3.7 Flash acceptances went from 15.5 to 68.5 percent. The four models gave the same decision on only 21 of 200 papers, and that decision was always major revision. The authors argue that an LLM should not be called a reviewer, because a reviewer recommends a decision and is accountable for the experiments requested, and that researchers should use LLMs only to check manuscripts for errors. The post also cites ScholarOne figures (33 percent more submissions in early 2026, and 4.5 invitations needed per review against 2.3 in 2018) and a 42 percent rise in submissions at Organization Science since ChatGPT. Because No. 032 already covered the preprint, use this only as a follow-up built on the framing result and the authors' argument, not as new coverage of the study. Curation agent to check whether the framing experiment is in the preprint or new in this post, to confirm the Substack's link to the journal, and to trace the ScholarOne and Organization Science figures to their sources.
