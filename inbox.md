- [Millennium Problems for biology](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/samuel-g-rodriques-080a9b22_in-light-of-the-progress-in-mathematics-share-7507215438561427456-C1Sc/)
  Samuel G. Rodriques (Edison Scientific, FutureHouse), LinkedIn, around 2026-09-19. A set of hard biology problems modelled on the Millennium Prize Problems, published at millenniumproblems.bio, led with Michaela Hinks. He frames it explicitly as a response to what has happened in mathematics, and calls it "the 'last reasonable eval' for AI in biology".
  The selection criteria are the interesting part and they are all about verification. Problems had to be "very hard to solve but very easy to validate in a simple laboratory environment", checkable in a standard wet lab in days to weeks, gradeable against an objective measurable endpoint, and understandable to someone with a basic biology background. Anything without a clear endpoint was excluded, ageing named as the example. Reviewers credited: Adam Marblestone, Michael Baym, Erika DeBenedictis, Tony Kulesa. 567 reactions and 37 comments when read.
  Relevance to the beat. This is the benchmark question moving from mathematics into biology, and it arrives in the same weeks as the argument about whether benchmarks are the right target at all. Edition 025 covered the declaration by 25 Fields Medallists calling the benchmark-driven approach "severely misaligned" with mathematics, and Tao's SAIR initiative in this inbox deliberately targets everyday mathematical work rather than benchmark problem-solving. Rodriques is moving the other way, building a benchmark on purpose, and the justification is that biology can validate an answer in a lab in a fortnight while mathematics has spent this month arguing about how a proof gets checked and credited. Whether cheap validation makes benchmarks safe, or just makes the same incentive problems arrive faster, is the question the edition can put next to the mathematics thread.
