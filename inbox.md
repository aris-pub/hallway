- [Summit on Math Research in the Age of AI: report](https://cmsa.fas.harvard.edu/aimathresearch_summit/)
  32 mathematicians met at Harvard's CMSA on 22 and 23 October (organizers Freed, Guth, Kra, Riehl, Abouzaid) and published recommendations on how AI is changing math research: credit and authorship for AI-assisted work, formalization in Lean as a supplement to the literature, training early-career mathematicians, and new publication formats (interactive articles, databases, venues that separate dissemination from validation). Names Hexagon and Palomar as new venues and the Bourgain Project, Erdős Problems and LMFDB as new ways to organize math. Report PDF: https://cmsa.fas.harvard.edu/media/2026/10/Math-research-summit-report.pdf

- [EMNLP 2026 discussion of AI reviewing and submission volume (Tanise Ceron)](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/taniseceron_emnlp2026-share-7521963028620394496-mHyb/)
  A LinkedIn post by Tanise Ceron reporting on AI-reviewing discussions at EMNLP 2026. - [How good is AI at molecular biology?](https://www.benchling.com/blog/how-good-is-ai-at-molecular-biology)
  Benchling (post by co-founder Ashu Singhal, October 30 2026) released BenchBench-Cloning with DNAForge, a benchmark of 65 molecular cloning tasks built with experts from real experiments. It covers two task types: protocol development and construct design. The top model, GPT-6 Astra, solves 30.6 percent, followed by GPT-6.1 Sol at 22.2 percent and Claude Opus 5.5 at 20.5 percent. Models often get individual steps right but fail to combine them, for example leaving out a control or designing primers that do not work together in one reaction. With ten attempts the top model solves 72 percent of protocol tasks, against 27 percent on one attempt. Fits with Terminal-Bench-Science (also in the inbox) as benchmarks built from real research workflows rather than exam questions. Benchling sells lab software, so note it is a vendor benchmark. - [30 Agents, 18 Pipelines, One Glass Box: Verifiable Agentic Science on Public Infrastructure](https://spond.github.io/CV.js/blog/2026-10-28-verifiable-agentic-science.html)
  An interactive explainer by Sergei Kosakovsky Pond for the bioRxiv preprint Baker et al. 2026 (doi 10.64898/2026.10.26.761864). Thirty frontier coding agents (10 each from Claude, Codex and Gemini) were each asked to plan a replication of one published bacterial RNA-seq study. 29 diverged from the paper and produced 18 distinct pipelines, and one common choice of reference genome erased a transcribed toxin gene. The authors then ran agents through UseGalaxy.org and free ACCESS-CI supercomputers, where every input, parameter and output is recorded. Doing so let them audit 48 published analyses. One audit found that a Paper2Agent result had been computed on a 1,000-cell test file bundled in the repository instead of the cited 5,613-cell atlas. Directly relevant to reproducibility of agent-run analyses and to who checks agent output. Curation agent to consider linking the bioRxiv preprint as the main URL, and to confirm the author list and figures from the preprint itself.

- [AI, Mathematics, Education, and a public goods problem](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/10/29/ai-mathematics-education-and-a-public-goods-problem/)
  A guest post on Terence Tao's blog (October 29 2026) by Gireeja Ranade, a professor in an EECS department. Ranade argues that society needs many trained mathematicians and scientists, not only the best few, and that AI weakens the incentives that used to produce them. Students tell her they have lost motivation, and fewer of her undergraduate researchers apply to PhD programs. She treats AI-assisted cheating as an early sign of a wider habit of signing off on work nobody fully understands: code that was not read, and eventually published results the authors do not understand. Verified Lean proofs move the problem to checking whether the theorem statements are faithful. She calls for teaching that holds students to the rigorous details rather than only the high-level ideas. Pairs with Terry Lyons on early-career researchers in No. 032 (Academy for the Mathematical Sciences), and with the Harvard CMSA summit report already in the inbox, which also covers training early-career mathematicians. Tao notes the post was converted to the blog format using AI. Curation agent to confirm Ranade's affiliation before naming it.

- [OpenResearch: autoresearch for arXiv papers (alphaXiv)](https://github.com/alphaXiv/OpenResearch)
  alphaXiv's open-source OpenResearch is a local desktop workspace for research agents. Its new feature lets a reader change "arxiv" to "autoarxiv" in any paper URL to have an agent set up a project and environment, then rerun the paper's experiments on compute the user picks, with logs, run charts and artifacts kept in one place. It works with any agent harness and any model, including open-weight models. Found through a LinkedIn launch post by Raj Palleti (https://www.linkedin.com/posts/raj-palleti_introducing-autoresearch-for-arxiv-papers-ugcPost-7521670303446511616-XSv7/), which says open models made up about one fifth of token volume that week. A commenter asked how often the reproduced runs match the paper's numbers, and the post does not say. That question is the useful angle, especially next to the Baker et al. preprint in the inbox, where 30 agents asked to replicate one RNA-seq study produced 18 different pipelines. Curation agent to check openresearch.sh for any reported reproduction rate, and to confirm Palleti's role at alphaXiv before naming them.

- [BioAnalyst: A Foundation Model for Biodiversity](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ecoinf.2026.104066)
  Published in Ecological Informatics by Thanasis Trantas and colleagues (TNO, BioDT project). BioAnalyst learns species and environmental conditions together from 10 kinds of data covering 20 years across Europe, including species occurrences, remote sensing, climate, soil and land use, at 0.25 degree resolution. It can be fine-tuned for different ecological and conservation planning tasks. The code (https://github.com/BioDT/bfm-model), weights, the BioCube dataset and the fine-tuning workflows are all public, and the authors ask others to test and challenge the results. The angle for the beat is the open release of a domain foundation model built by scientists in a field other than ML. In a LinkedIn post (https://www.linkedin.com/posts/trantas_ai-foundationmodels-biodiversity-share-7522814492355739648-GoSd/), Trantas also argues that a small share of the money spent on LLM benchmark gains would do more good if spent on AI for science with societal goals. - [Talk with my publications! (Niels G. Mede)](https://chat.nielsmede.com/)
  Niels Mede, an assistant professor of science communication, built a chat assistant over their own 50 publications. Readers can ask questions of one paper or all of them, and for the 26 papers with data, including the TISP survey of trust in scientists across 68 countries, it runs analyses and draws plots. It uses Mistral Large, an open-weight model served from EU data centers, and the model receives only aggregate results, not the raw data. Answers cite page numbers and link to the page in the PDF for the 44 open-access papers, and the page says the analyses have not been reviewed by the authors. Found through Mede's LinkedIn post (https://www.linkedin.com/posts/ngmede_ai-tisp-mistral-share-7522999164687863809-PY-P/), where commenters ask how it differs from NotebookLM, raise the risk that LLM summaries flatten what a reader would have noticed alone, and ask whether this could replace the fixed-length paper as the way findings reach readers. Fits the theme of papers becoming something readers query rather than read (Paper2Agent, the OpenResearch entry in the inbox), here built by an author for their own work.
