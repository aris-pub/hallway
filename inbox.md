- [Fast, but incomplete and inaccurate: fifteen AI-powered tools for literature search](https://renebekkers.wordpress.com/2026/10/10/fast-but-incomplete-and-inaccurate-twelve-ai-powered-tools-for-literature-search/)
  René Bekkers (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam, research on charitable giving) tested AI literature search tools against a known answer. A team of six scholars had found 48 replications of experiments on charitable giving by searching Google Scholar by hand. Bekkers then asked each tool to find them. The best tool, Google Scholar Labs, found about one third. Most tools, including Perplexity, found fewer than 10, and Elsevier's LeapSpace returned false positives for up to 70 percent of its results. All fifteen tools together found half of the 48. Longer prompts with explanations and examples did not improve the results. The tools did find some real replications that the manual search had missed, because those papers used other terms. The post first covered twelve tools on October 10, and Bekkers added three more on November 15 (two of them found one and zero of the 48). The paper, with prompts, data and code, is on OSF (https://osf.io/ghxn3/files/yqaju). Bekkers will present it at the Leibniz Association Open Science day in Berlin and then submit it to Open Research Europe, so it has not been peer reviewed. Found through Bekkers's LinkedIn post (https://www.linkedin.com/posts/rene-bekkers-6ab2162_update-i-tested-three-more-ai-powered-tools-share-7527722379943387136-Z7Sd/), in which Bekkers asks the tool makers to respond. This is a rare test with a known answer, so recall and false positives can both be measured. - [MoltJournal: an experimental scientific institution for autonomous researchers](https://moltjournal.org/?view=human)
  MoltJournal is a preprint server built by Petter Törnberg (started at the University of Amsterdam) in which AI agents submit, review, edit and revise papers under five rules, and the software enforces those rules without an editor-in-chief. Submitting costs credits, and agents earn credits only by writing reviews that an editor marks as adequate, so publishing requires reviewing. Soundness and significance are judged separately. Panels are drawn from different operators and, where possible, from different model families, and the site lists, for each paper, how independent its reviewers were. Published papers stay open to later review and correction. A person or lab registers as the operator with an academic email address and is accountable for everything that its agents do. Every submission, review and decision goes into a public event log that anyone can download, and Törnberg plans to analyse that log to find out which rules lead to a reliable record. The design is described in a forthcoming paper, "Institutions for Machine Science". When fetched, the site had no submitted papers yet. This pairs with the LLM peer review items (Le Guellec and Kim, the EMNLP AI-reviewing discussion) as a test of what rules would make AI review reliable, rather than a test of AI review inside the current system. Link the ?view=human page, because the bare URL serves instructions written for agents. Curation agent to confirm the launch date, whether any papers have been published since, and whether the "Institutions for Machine Science" paper is out.

- [AI agents on both sides of protein design](https://yshen25.github.io/anthropic-adaptyv-protein-design-competition/articles/02-agents-and-selection.html)
  The second of three articles (November 2026) by Yue (Shawn) Shen on taking part in the Anthropic and Adaptyv protein design competition with a colleague. They split the agent work into three roles: a coordinator that proposed hypotheses and plans, an executor that ran analyses and kept rerunnable records of inputs, settings and failures, and a reviewer that checked claims against evidence. The two researchers directed the work and made the final decisions. The team saved a task queue, checkpoints and reviewer findings in project files, so that a fresh agent session could continue from those files instead of from chat memory. Shen reports that a separate reviewer role helped but did not guarantee correctness, because agents shared assumptions and sometimes spent too long repairing tools. Shen then looks at the other use of AI in the competition: under a selection prompt that the organizers published, Claude is used to recommend which submissions get wet-lab testing, and the organizers approve the choice. Shen notes that the published prompt contains only a policy, with no documentation of the software that runs it. Shen plans to cover the cases where the researchers stepped in in Article 3. Found through Shen's LinkedIn post (https://www.linkedin.com/posts/yue-shawn-shen_how-should-we-organize-ai-agents-for-scientific-share-7527765753987563520-wtR9/). Article 1 is about the five protein targets. This is a first-hand account of how a small team organized agents in real research, and it pairs with the World Bank DIME code-review skill and the Baker et al. provenance preprint in the inbox. - [A Society of AI Scientists](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2611.society-ai-scientists)
  A report posted to alphaXiv on November 19 2026 by Samuel Schmidgall, Rehaan Ahmad and more than 40 co-authors (MIT, Stanford, ETH Zurich, CMU, JHU, UCSF and other institutions). It describes a twelve-week study in which AI agents run by 35 human-operated labs worked in a shared preprint server and chat workspace. According to the abstract, the agents produced 936 studies with code and data and exchanged 9,500 messages in 229 chat rooms that agents created. Agents publicly retracted mistaken claims, reported negative results about as often as positive ones, and agents in one lab set up the Journal of Agent Research, whose agent editors inspected code. In blinded comparisons with recent human preprints, raters scored the agent studies higher on rigor and on measured claims, and scored the human preprints higher on novelty and significance. The full archive of papers, code, data, reviews and chat logs is released. alphaXiv is opening the agent preprint server to the public on the same day, and alphaXiv also hosts the report (marked "alphaXiv Exclusive", so not on arXiv), so the platform's operator published the study of its own platform. Found through Schmidgall's LinkedIn post (https://www.linkedin.com/posts/samuel-schmidgall-288632162_excited-to-share-new-research-across-35-ugcPost-7529265327210434562-aMm8/). Pairs directly with MoltJournal in the inbox (an agent-run journal with fixed rules) and with alphaXiv's OpenResearch entry. - [On AI for Quantitative Social Science](https://posts.carlislerainey.com/p/on-ai-for-quantitative-social-science)
  An essay (November 16 2026) by political scientist Carlisle Rainey, framed as early thoughts. Rainey treats the code that turns a dataset into a paper as the main research product and asks that it be principled, implemented and documented. On that view, coding agents clearly help with implementing and documenting choices, and Rainey argues that researchers should at least use them for error-checking. Two real examples of errors that humans rarely catch: a paper that gives a prior as N(0, 2) meaning a variance while the Stan code reads 2 as a standard deviation, and a C++ implementation that differs subtly from the algorithm described in the paper. Rainey names two mistakes. Researchers may use AI too little and miss errors that it would find, or use it too much, either on judgments that need a human (whether causal assumptions are credible, which questions matter) or on cheap work that should not be done at all, such as data collection that once had to be justified because it was expensive. Rainey's expectation is that agents will let researchers work more carefully, not faster. Pairs directly with the World Bank DIME post on reviewing AI-written research code, which cites the same kind of silent errors. Curation agent to confirm Rainey's affiliation and the "one to six scripts and 500 to 2,500 lines" figure, which comes from Rainey's quick look at replication archives and not from a study.

- [NVIDIA Introduces Nemotron for Science to Accelerate Discoveries With Agents](https://nvidianews.nvidia.com/news/nvidia-introduces-nemotron-for-science-to-accelerate-discoveries-with-agents)
  An NVIDIA press release (November 16 2026). Nemotron for Science bundles the open Nemotron 3.5 Super VL model with an open chemistry dataset, scientific evaluations and post-training recipes, so that research groups can adapt the model to their own tasks and run it on their own hardware. The dataset, LitmusBench v0.2 (https://huggingface.co/datasets/nvidia/Nemotron-RL-litmus-bench-v0.2), has nearly 50,000 chemistry questions on molecular properties and drug discovery data, for both training and evaluation. NVIDIA reports results from partner companies. Terray Therapeutics post-trained the model to pull reactions and conditions out of published diagrams, recovering about 76 percent of annotated reactions on the RxnScribe test. Lila Sciences reports that post-training with feedback from its automated lab nearly doubled the model's accuracy on quantum dot synthesis protocols to 61 percent, against 54 percent for GPT-6 Astra, at much lower cost. Argonne and Jülich Supercomputing Centre are named as users. Found through a LinkedIn post by Javier Medel (https://www.linkedin.com/posts/javier-medel_nvidia-introduced-nemotron-for-science-share-7530136486642339840-n6wk/). For the beat, the angle is open weights and open training data that a lab can adapt and run itself, in contrast with the closed science offerings already in the inbox and recent editions (AlphaGenome 2 behind a non-commercial API, Antigravity for science). All performance figures come from NVIDIA and its partners, not from independent tests. Curation agent to say so in the item, and to check whether any of the partner results have a paper or preprint.

- [G.R.H. Polymath](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/11/24/g-r-h-polymath/)
  A guest post on Terence Tao's blog (November 24 2026) by Anurag Sahay, Will Sawin and Lior Silberman, proposing a Polymath project around Levent Alpöge's Lean-only proof of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis. The project has four goals: put the checking and simplification efforts that are now running separately in one place, produce a proof that humans can read and understand, sort out the related literature, and study the new mathematics in the proof and more general versions of it. The organizers expect the work to split into subprojects, because the proof uses many fields inside and outside number theory. Discussion happens on a public Zulip forum (https://rh-polymath.zulipchat.com) that anyone can read without logging in, and any review or research papers would follow the Polymath custom of a group pseudonym. No. 035 led with the claim itself, so this is a follow-up: mathematicians are organizing to turn a formal proof that no one understands into mathematics that people can read, which is new work that the AI release created.
