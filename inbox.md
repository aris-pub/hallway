- [More than half of recent astronomy papers are written with language-model assistance](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.10664)
  Serat M. Saad and Yuan-Sen Ting, arXiv 2609.10664, submitted September 9 2026. Analyses 207,111 astronomy papers from 2015 to mid-2026 using vocabulary markers left by language models, with a hierarchical Bayesian estimate of assisted versus unassisted writing. Headline numbers: 54 percent of 2025 papers carry detectable traces, statistical uncertainty plus or minus 8 percent and systematic uncertainty +26/-0, while only 0.81 percent of 2025 papers disclose language model use. That is roughly one disclosure for every 66 papers showing a trace. The authors also find the markers more than halve between 2023 and 2026, which they read as authors adapting their language away from detectable patterns.
  Relevance to the beat. This is the empirical version of the disclosure argument the newsletter has been covering through policy statements and individual incidents. A 66 to 1 gap between use and disclosure says the norms currently being drafted are already far behind practice in at least one field. The declining detectability finding may matter more than the headline, because it means the measurement method has a shelf life and any policy resting on detection is built on ground that is moving. Curation agent to confirm the arXiv ID resolves and has not been updated or withdrawn, verify both author affiliations, and state the +26/-0 systematic uncertainty precisely, since that asymmetry is unusual and easy to garble.

- [Editors of higher education journal coauthor editorial with fake references](https://retractionwatch.com/2026/09/08/editors-of-higher-education-journal-coauthor-editorial-with-fake-references/)
  Retraction Watch, September 8 2026. Editors of a higher education journal published an editorial in which at least nine of 31 references are nonexistent, name real scholars against articles they did not write, or attach real journal titles to the wrong authors. Brendan Cantwell of Michigan State University, who studies higher education, had a fabricated paper on academic freedom in Higher Education Policy attributed to him, and told Retraction Watch "The cited article is not something that I wrote and is presumably a fabricated citation." Taylor and Francis says its publishing ethics and integrity team is investigating.
  Relevance to the beat. Named, concrete, and unusually close to home, because the people who did not check the citations are the editors, which is the role the system relies on to catch exactly this. Most fabricated-citation coverage is about authors gaming review. This one is about the gatekeepers. It sits well beside the astronomy disclosure paper above, since both describe the same gap between what AI is doing to the literature and what the people responsible for the literature are actually checking. Curation agent to confirm the journal name and the editors' names, which I did not verify, check whether the editorial has since been corrected or retracted or the investigation concluded, and confirm the Cantwell quote against the article text.

- [Guest Post: The Unbundling of Scholarly Publishing, A Trilemma for the AI Era](https://scholarlykitchen.sspnet.org/2026/09/09/guest-post-the-unbundling-of-scholarly-publishing-a-trilemma-for-the-ai-era/)
  The Scholarly Kitchen, guest post, September 9 2026. Argues that a journal portfolio, platform or membership is no longer sufficient on its own, because AI changes how research is discovered and consumed: readers get answers without ever visiting the platform that certified the work. The proposed response is that provenance and attribution have to travel with the answer rather than sit on the platform.
  Relevance to the beat. Structural rather than incident-driven, which the edition mix usually needs alongside the disputes. It is also the publishing-industry mirror of the argument the mathematicians are having, since in both cases the complaint is that the artifact travels while the attribution does not. - [Arthur Turrell on the Google and Google DeepMind study of AI in science, led by Mihai Codreanu](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/arthur-turrell-8b789891_today-at-google-google-deepmind-we-published-share-7505646672472018945-y0D_/)
  Arthur Turrell, LinkedIn, mid September 2026, summarising a paper he co-authored. The evidence base is unusually large: 15 million Gemini interactions, 2,600 specialised AI models, and a survey of 600 scientists. The economic framing is the reason the numbers are being collected. If AI is a one-off boost you get a step change, but if it is "an innovation in the method of innovation" you get a permanently higher growth rate, and science is where you would see that first.
  Findings as Turrell states them. Scientists are about 2.7 times overrepresented in AI use relative to their employment share. Science conversations are longer, more multimodal, and involve more domain expertise, with computer science the heaviest user. Specialised models and LLMs act as complements rather than substitutes: LLMs handle code, statistics, literature, drafting, teaching and admin, while specialised models predict, generate and classify, for instance protein structures and weather. Within LLM use, analysis and modelling and communicating research findings are the top two tasks. On outcomes, 74 percent of surveyed scientists report net time savings against 6 percent reporting losses, averaging about 7 hours a week, mostly reinvested in more research, and 47 percent report output increases of 10 percent or more.
  The part worth leading with. The bottleneck has moved rather than disappeared. 89 percent of those saving time spend more than a tenth of it checking AI outputs, and 41 percent report a growing backlog of untested hypotheses. Turrell's own forecast is that time on bottleneck tasks grows as a share, papers increasingly recombine ideas across disciplines, and publication volume rises fastest in fields that need no wet lab work, with the caveat that not all of it will be good, which he calls "sci-slop". His summary is that the potential is significant and 7 hours a week is already real, but verification bottlenecks stop it becoming a productivity take-off.
  Relevance to the beat. This is the first quantitative picture of AI use in science at this scale, and it lands against a run of edition items that were qualitative or single-incident. It pairs directly with the astronomy disclosure paper already in this inbox: that one measured how much AI writing is happening and how little is disclosed, this one measures what scientists say they get from it and where the time goes. The verification-bottleneck finding is also the empirical version of what the Fields Medalists declaration argues from principle, that mass production of results outruns the human capacity to absorb them. "Sci-slop" plus the 41 percent untested-hypothesis backlog is a concrete mechanism for that.
  Curation agent to confirm: the actual paper rather than the LinkedIn summary. Turrell links it at https://lnkd.in/g55s4MtC which needs resolving. Get the full author list (Mihai Codreanu leads, Turrell is a co-author, the post says a large team), the venue and date, whether it is peer reviewed or a preprint, and check every number above against the paper rather than the post. Also confirm Turrell's affiliation, since the post says "at Google and Google DeepMind" without saying which employs him.

- [Reimagining research papers as interactive and reliable AI agents](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-11044-y)
  Jiacheng Miao, Joe R. Davis, Yaohui Zhang, Jonathan K. Pritchard and James Zou, Nature, published online 2026-09-16. Introduces Paper2Agent, a framework that converts a research paper into an AI agent. It analyses the paper and its codebase with multiple agents, builds a Model Context Protocol server exposing the manuscript, supplementary materials, datasets, code and workflows, then generates and runs tests to harden it. The resulting server connects to a chat agent such as Claude Code, so a reader asks questions in natural language and the agent invokes the paper's own tools and workflows. The authors call it "a virtual corresponding author" and describe the shift as turning research output "from passive artefacts into active systems".
  Case studies. An agent built on AlphaGenome to interpret genomic variants, and agents built on Scanpy and TISSUE for single-cell and spatial transcriptomics. They validate that the agents reproduce the original papers' results and also answer new user queries. In one case several paper agents collaborate to prioritise a causal gene for psoriasis.
  Relevance to the beat. This is the strongest statement yet of a position the newsletter has been circling: that the barrier to reuse is the paper being static, and the fix is to make it executable. It belongs in an edition because of who is saying it (Nature, with Pritchard and Zou attached) rather than because the idea is new.
  Worth drawing out the disagreement rather than reporting it flat. Paper2Agent's diagnosis is the same one that motivates web-native publishing generally, that a static document buries the method. Its prescription is the opposite: instead of making the artifact itself legible to a human reader, it wraps the artifact in a machine that answers on its behalf. The reader stops reading the paper and starts querying a proxy for it. That runs directly into two things already in this inbox. The Scholarly Kitchen unbundling piece warned that readers increasingly get answers without visiting what certified the work. The Fields Medalists declaration argued that what keeps ideas alive is the human transmission chain, mathematicians developing and integrating each other's work, and that mass-produced true-or-false output threatens it. A paper that answers questions on your behalf is a clean instance of both worries, and the paper does not appear to engage with either.
  Curation agent to confirm: the full text, which is paywalled (the abstract above is from the citation metadata, not the article). Check whether the authors address provenance, error propagation or what happens when the agent is wrong, and whether there is any discussion of the reader losing contact with the original argument. Get affiliations for all five authors (Pritchard and Zou are Stanford, verify the rest). Check whether Paper2Agent is released as code and under what licence, and whether the generated MCP servers are hosted anywhere or run locally. Also check the article type, since Nature research article versus perspective changes how to frame it.

- [alphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/)
  A discovery and discussion layer built over arXiv, founded May 2023 by Rehaan Ahmad and Raj Palleti out of a Stanford web development class. The original feature was a comment button next to paragraphs of an arXiv PDF, and the access pattern is still that you swap arxiv.org for alphaxiv.org in a paper URL to get the paper and its discussion side by side. It has since grown well past commenting: researcher profiles and following, literature review tooling, engagement metrics on papers, an MCP server, a browser extension, and an "Autoresearch" tool at openresearch.sh. It raised 7 million dollars in seed funding co-led by Menlo Ventures and Haystack, with Eric Schmidt and Sebastian Thrun among the angels.
  Relevance to the beat. Two things make this worth an item rather than a mention. First, it is the clearest case of the reading layer migrating off the archive. arXiv hosts the paper, alphaXiv owns the conversation about it, the metrics, and increasingly the interface a reader actually uses. That is the Scholarly Kitchen unbundling argument already in this inbox, happening in public, with venture funding behind it.
  Second, the MCP server. Paper2Agent, the Nature paper also in this inbox, turns an individual paper into an agent. alphaXiv is doing the same thing one level up, exposing the whole corpus and its discussion as agent-callable. Taken together they describe a reading layer where neither the human nor the archive is in the loop: the agent queries the corpus, and the paper is the thing at the far end that nobody opens. Worth naming that convergence explicitly, since the two arrived within days of each other.
  Curation agent to confirm: whether the 7 million round closed in November 2025 or 2026, since the coverage is ambiguous and it changes how current this is. What the MCP server actually exposes and whether it covers all of arXiv or a subset. What Autoresearch does, since openresearch.sh is a separate property. Whether arXiv itself has any relationship with or position on alphaXiv, which matters for the unbundling framing. Current user numbers if any are published, and whether the paper view counts shown on the site are alphaXiv views or arXiv views.
