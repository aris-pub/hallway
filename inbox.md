- [The Denario project: Deep knowledge AI agents for scientific discovery](https://journals.aps.org/prxintelligence/abstract/10.1103/kk55-gc95)
  Published in PRX Intelligence (APS), reported as 2026-10-01. Preprint at arXiv:2510.26887 (October 2025), code at github.com/Denario-AI/Denario, project page at astropilot-ai.github.io/DenarioPaperPage. Handed over as Thomas Meier's LinkedIn post (linkedin.com/posts/thomas-meier-340721203_denario-project-share-7510982345999405056-cA56), posted around 2026-10-02, which notes support from the Munich Center for Machine Learning. More than 35 authors across astrophysics, biology, biophysics, chemistry, materials science, mathematics, medicine, neuroscience, quantum physics, machine learning and philosophy. Reported leads are Boris Bolliet (Cambridge), Pablo Villanueva Domingo (Autonomous University of Barcelona) and Francisco Villaescusa-Navarro.
  What it is. A modular multi-agent system meant to cover the whole research workflow: generating ideas, checking literature, planning the research, writing and running code, making plots, and drafting and reviewing the paper. Built on AG2 and LangGraph, with Cmbagent as a deep-research backend for near end-to-end analysis. The paper demonstrates it by including AI-generated papers in a long list of fields. Meier's own contribution is on the epistemic side, responsibility attribution and research integrity, and he opens his post by asking whether an AI can do science "or only produce papers that look like science".
  Relevance to the beat. The news is the venue. A full-workflow paper-writing agent system has now been through peer review at an APS journal, which is different from the preprint-and-press-release pattern most of these systems follow, including ScientistTwo in a recent edition. Two threads meet here. One is the volume and review load already covered through the arXiv submission counts and the new arXiv rate limit, since a system whose demonstration output is a set of generated papers is on the supply side of exactly that problem. The other is attribution, which this project at least takes seriously enough to put a philosopher on the author list, and which bears on the astronomy disclosure gap in edition 026 and on the Advisory Group's work on how machine-produced results get presented. Also worth noting that a 35-author paper listing AI-generated papers as results is itself an authorship question.

- [Scientific Agents: Evaluating Profession-Specific System Prompts on Scientific Tasks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.00084)
  Timothy Kassis (K-Dense Inc.), arXiv:2610.00084, v1. Categories cs.AI and cs.CL. 46 pages, 11 of main text with a 33-page appendix, 10 figures and 29 tables. Prompt corpus released at github.com/K-Dense-AI/scientific-agents. Handed over as the author's LinkedIn post (linkedin.com/posts/timothykassis_we-built-503-expert-scientist-prompts-for-share-7511814537415729152-ODkI), posted around 2026-10-03.
  What it reports, and it is a negative result. The author built an open corpus of 503 profession-specific system prompts for scientific work, then tested them against a plain "You are a helpful assistant" baseline. Across nine text-based science benchmarks and 4,531 questions, the average difference was -0.6 percentage points, and the paper states that "no benchmark shows a statistically clear improvement". The expert prompts used 1.5 to 2.3 times more output tokens and cost 2.2 to 4.5 times more per call. On bioinformatics tool-use tasks the baseline solved 56.7 percent against 46.7 percent with the profiles, which the paper attributes to long prompts crowding out the work inside the token limit. One odd result reported in the post is that the longer prompts did better when API requests were dropping, 71.6 percent against 54.0 percent correct. A commenter called the general effect a "credential tax", meaning tokens spent establishing qualifications rather than doing the task.
  Relevance to the beat. Most of the recent items are systems that claim to do research, including Denario and ScientistTwo, and this one tests a cheap practice that nearly everyone building such systems uses, then reports that it does not work. Persona prompting is in the methods section of a great deal of applied work right now, usually without any evidence behind it, so a measured null result with cost numbers attached is directly useful to anyone building these pipelines. It also fits the verification thread from edition 026, where the time AI saves gets spent checking outputs, since this is a case of the checking being done and the answer coming back negative. The author releasing the corpus and then publishing the evidence that it does not help is worth one sentence on its own.
  Curation agent to confirm, and the limits matter here: the submission date, because the arXiv identifier says October 2026 while the listing read as September 7 2026, so check the abstract page. Whether Kassis is the only author and what K-Dense Inc. builds, since a company making scientific agents publishing a null result about a common technique is context the reader should have. That only one model was tested, Gemini 3.8 Flash through OpenRouter, which limits how far the result generalises, and whether any other model appears in the appendix. That the abstract says evaluation code and item-level records are not released, which should be stated plainly if the item runs. Which nine benchmarks were used, and what BioMysteryBench and SuperGPQA are. And whether the API-drop result is in the paper or only in the post.

- [Carbon-A: Finding genes in known and unknown genomes](https://huggingface.co/blog/HuggingFaceBio/carbon-annotator-genbank-genome-annotation)
  Georgia Channing with Leandro von Werra and the Gener Team, published under HuggingFaceBio, 2026-10-08. Handed over as Thomas Wolf's LinkedIn post (linkedin.com/posts/thom-wolf_scientists-have-sequenced-the-genomes-of-ugcPost-7513986966506405888-ghKV), posted around 2026-10-08. Wet-lab work with ActiveSite and UCSD, annotations to be distributed by EMBL-EBI, where Fergal Martin of the Eukaryotic Annotation Team is quoted. Weights at HuggingFaceBio/Carbon-A-1.2B, database explorer at huggingface.co/spaces/HuggingFaceBio/genbank-annotation-explorer, technical report in the Hub collection.
  What it is. A 1.2 billion parameter model that predicts protein-coding regions straight from DNA, one shared model across mammals, other vertebrates, invertebrates, plants, fungi and protists, with a 98,304 base pair context window and per-nucleotide predictions on both strands. The argument for it is that sequencing is now cheap while annotation is the bottleneck, so most sequenced species have no usable gene annotations. Reported results: macro-averaged nucleotide F1 of 0.944 across 42 benchmark genomes, ahead of AUGUSTUS, Helixer, Tiberius, ANNEVO, OrionGeno, SegmentNT and NTv3 at nucleotide, exon and gene level, and 0.960 on Tetrahymena thermophila, which uses a nonstandard genetic code. The released Carbon Annotation Database covers 48,167 assemblies from 22,617 taxa, about 27 trillion base pairs and 566 million predicted protein-coding loci, which the post puts at 11 times more taxa and 9 times more sequence than the RefSeq corpus it was trained on. PacBio Iso-Seq experiments in cat, Syrian hamster, chicken and Arabidopsis supported predictions missing from RefSeq, with the post reporting 239 candidate genes found that way.
  What it does not claim, which is the reason to run it. The scope is stated rather than implied. The model marks where genes are and does not design DNA or predict function. On the RNA evidence the authors write "they do not yet establish that those RNAs are translated into proteins", and they say proteomics, mass spectrometry and ribosome profiling are still needed. They also note the decoder "still produces a single consensus path per locus", so isoforms are unresolved, and they report a case where the model scored better against a 2026 pig assembly than against the 2017 assembly it was trained on, which they read as having learned coding patterns rather than memorised labels.
  Relevance to the beat. Put this next to the Anthropic phage enzyme announcement from two weeks ago. Both are machine-found biology with human lab work behind them, and the difference is what was released and what was claimed. Here the weights, the training data, the database and a technical report are public, a public archive is distributing the annotations, and the authors spell out what the evidence does not show. That makes it the practical version of what the ICIAM statement asked for, open validation environments and disclosure of computational methods and resources, and it belongs alongside the open-weights thread running through Tao's SAIR initiative. It also inverts the usual discovery framing, since the claim is not that the model found something true but that it produced 566 million hypotheses with a confidence score attached and left the checking to everyone else.
  Curation agent to confirm: the licence on the model and the database, which the page does not state and which decides whether "open" is the right word. Whether the technical report is a preprint with authors and a DOI or only a Hub document, since that changes how to cite it. The 239 candidate genes figure, which comes from Wolf's post rather than the blog. What the 0.62 complete-CDS support number means next to the reference, since that is the one validation figure quoted. Channing's and von Werra's affiliations and what the Gener Team is, and Wolf's role, since he promoted it but is not in the byline. Whether EMBL-EBI has said anything itself beyond the quote. And whether any outside group has evaluated the database, since the comparisons are all from the authors.

- [AHM Statement on OpenAI's October 6 Release of Mathematical Documents](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/10/07/ahm-statement-on-openais-october-6-release-of-mathematical-documents/)
  Guest post on Terence Tao's blog, 2026-10-07, reposted from the Association for Human Mathematics statements page at ahmath.org/statements. Signed only by the "Association for Human Mathematics Communications Working Group", with no individual named. Handed over as an aggregator post on LinkedIn (linkedin.com/posts/ai-mathematics-openai-share-7514097435158343680-cTyI) from a page called The Algorithm AI. Do not cite that post.
