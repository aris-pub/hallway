A paper this week from Baker, Nekrutenko, Kosakovsky Pond, and colleagues gave thirty frontier AI agents the same task: replicate one published bacterial RNA-seq study. They produced eighteen incompatible pipelines. One common choice of reference genome silently erased a transcribed toxin gene from the analysis.

The fix the authors propose is worth noticing. They moved the agents onto public infrastructure (UseGalaxy.org and free ACCESS-CI compute), where every input, parameter, and output is recorded automatically. That made it possible to audit 48 published analyses and find that one result had been computed on a 1,000-cell test file bundled in the repository rather than the cited 5,613-cell atlas.

The problem is not that agents make mistakes. It is that agent-run analyses currently produce no audit trail unless someone designs one in. The public infrastructure approach is one answer: not slower, not more manual, just observable.

This week's edition also covers AutoDNA (AI agents running a full DNA lab with humans only restocking reagents), a wave of seven essays from mathematicians on what publishing and credit should look like when results arrive faster than anyone can read them, and a benchmark showing frontier models assemble molecular biology steps correctly but fail to combine them into valid protocols.
