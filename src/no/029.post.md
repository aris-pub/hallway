A survey of 637 scientists finds that nearly half have shifted toward safer, incremental questions since adopting AI tools. The survey is from Google DeepMind, using data from 15 million Gemini interactions and a sample of their own users. The company measuring the narrowing is the company selling the thing being measured.

This is not a reason to dismiss the finding. The numbers are specific: 48.8 percent shifted toward incremental work, with the tendency most concentrated among junior researchers. 45.7 percent now spend more than a quarter of their AI-saved time auditing AI output. A separate study linked from the essay found that AI-enabled researchers publish more and get more citations, while the collective range of inquiry contracts.

What it adds up to is a picture of individual rationality producing collective narrowing. Researchers are making sensible choices given how reward systems work. The output is a discipline where the questions being asked are safer than they were.

The essay argues for public investment in verification infrastructure and peer review reform. That seems right. But the fact that one company is now in a position to publish this data about its own product's effect on science, and to write the policy case for fixing the downstream effects, is itself part of the picture.
