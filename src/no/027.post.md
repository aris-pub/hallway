The Millennium Problems for biology were published this week at millenniumproblems.bio. There are twelve of them, modelled on the Clay Institute's Millennium Prize Problems. The interesting part is not the list but the selection rule.

Every problem had to be very hard to solve but checkable against an objective measurable endpoint in a standard wet lab within days to weeks. Anything without a clear validation criterion was excluded. The framing from the organizers is explicit: this is meant to be the last reasonable evaluation benchmark for AI in biology, and the design embeds a specific claim, that a benchmark is only sound if a human can independently verify the answer without specialized infrastructure or years of follow-up.

That criterion matters because the week's other major item points in a different direction. A multi-agent framework described in a new arXiv paper runs the full scientific cycle autonomously and evaluates the results using AI reviewers, benchmarked against papers accepted at ICLR, ICML, and NeurIPS. The headline finding is that papers produced by the system score higher under that evaluation than human-authored ones. The evaluation is AI reviewing AI.

Both projects are serious attempts to answer the same question: what does it mean for a machine-produced scientific result to be valid? One answer is lab validation by a human. The other is peer review by another model. The edition has more on both, plus the mathematics community forming an advisory body specifically to handle the case where neither standard has been agreed on yet.
